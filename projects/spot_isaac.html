Overview

Autonomy where
humans can't go

After earthquakes, explosions, or structural failures, first responders face environments that are unstable, GPS-denied, poorly lit, and immediately dangerous. Wheeled robots struggle with debris and elevation changes. Aerial systems face endurance limits indoors.


Legged robots offer a compelling alternative — but bridging learned locomotion with reliable navigation planning in these environments remains an open systems problem. This project works toward that bridge.

Simulation
Autonomous indoor navigation — Isaac Sim, disaster-inspired environment, ROS 2 Nav2
Problem

Disaster
environments

The operating conditions for urban search and rescue push every component of a robotic system to its limits simultaneously.

  • Collapsed or partially damaged indoor structures
  • Narrow corridors, doorways, and stairwells
  • Debris fields and irregular, uneven surfaces
  • No GPS — localisation must be entirely onboard
  • Low and variable lighting conditions
  • Time pressure — every second of situational awareness matters
Step 01 — Locomotion

Learning to
walk first

Rather than hand-engineering gaits, a reinforcement learning policy was trained in Isaac Lab to learn walking behavior directly from environment interaction. This produces smooth, disturbance-tolerant locomotion that can handle moderate terrain irregularities without explicit gait programming.


The locomotion policy serves as a reliable foundation layer — abstracting away low-level joint control so that higher-level navigation can issue velocity commands without worrying about how the legs execute them.

Isaac Lab (RL training environment) ↓ Reward shaping — stability & smooth motion — forward velocity tracking — disturbance tolerance — recovery from perturbations ↓ Trained locomotion policy — maps velocity commands → joint torques — robust to moderate terrain variation ↓ Velocity command interface — linear x/y, angular z — compatible with ROS 2 Nav2 cmd_vel
Step 02 — Navigation

Classical planning
on learned motion

With locomotion in place, Isaac Sim was used to construct disaster-inspired indoor environments and connect the robot to the ROS 2 Nav2 stack. The goal: get a classical path planner to issue commands that a learned locomotion policy can faithfully execute.


This integration surfaced real systems challenges — sensor synchronization, velocity command compatibility between planners and legged motion, and navigation stability in tight, cluttered spaces. Each one required careful tuning of the Nav2 costmaps and controller parameters.

  • Isaac Sim — disaster-inspired indoor environment construction
  • ROS 2 Nav2 — global and local path planning
  • SLAM-based localisation — no GPS dependency
  • Sensor synchronisation between simulation and ROS 2 bridge
  • Costmap tuning for narrow corridors and tight doorways
  • Velocity command bridging — Nav2 cmd_vel → locomotion policy
  • Goal-directed autonomy across multiple waypoints
System

Full autonomy
stack

The pipeline deliberately separates locomotion from navigation — each layer designed and validated independently before being composed together. This modularity makes failures easier to isolate and each component swappable.

ENVIRONMENT (Isaac Sim) — disaster-inspired indoor scenes — debris, narrow corridors, low light ↓ PERCEPTION — onboard LIDAR + depth camera — SLAM localisation (GPS-denied) — occupancy map building ↓ NAVIGATION (ROS 2 Nav2) — global planner: A* / Dijkstra — local planner: DWB controller — costmap: inflation + obstacle layers ↓ VELOCITY COMMANDS (cmd_vel) ↓ LOCOMOTION POLICY (Isaac Lab RL) — velocity tracking — stability & recovery ↓ JOINT COMMANDS → quadruped hardware
Status
Active — work in progress

Foundation built.
Challenges remain.

Autonomous indoor navigation is functional in simulation. The system reliably reaches navigation goals across moderately cluttered environments. Several challenges remain before deployment-ready autonomy can be claimed.

  • Robustness under severe terrain variation and step obstacles
  • Reliable perception in very low-visibility environments
  • Longer-horizon autonomy and failure recovery behaviors
  • Tighter integration between Nav2 replanning and locomotion limits
  • Sim-to-real validation on physical Spot hardware
