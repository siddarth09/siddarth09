Measured the robot.
Then trained on it.

A walking policy is only as good as the simulator it was trained in. So I measured a real Unitree G1's leg joints, found the largest error was a parameter the standard tooling never fits, fixed it, and trained on the corrected model. The robot walks, and I drive it from the keyboard.

The idea

The simulator already knows the robot's shape and mass — that comes from CAD. What it doesn't know is what happens inside each joint: the motor's own spinning inertia, amplified by a gearbox; a viscous resistance that grows with speed; and dry friction that fights you the same whether you move fast or slow.

You can't measure any of that with a meter, because it's a sealed gearbox. So you wiggle the robot in a pattern you designed, record what its joints actually did, then make the simulator perform the same wiggle and adjust the numbers until simulated motion matches the recording. You're not measuring the parameters; you're finding the values that make the model reproduce reality.

The thing the pipeline doesn't fit

Identification tools fit rotor inertia, damping and friction, and treat the actuator gain as a known constant. It isn't. I cross-checked the actuator model by regressing the robot's own reported torque against the commanded Kp (q_des − q) − Kd q̇ — the one signal in the dataset that doesn't come from the position trajectory the fit was already minimising.

The robot delivers 76–95% of the torque the model assumes. Correcting it moved held-out error more than rotor inertia, damping and friction combined — a 30× cost reduction on a single-joint fit, against 6% from all three dynamics parameters together.

The root cause was in the gain design: Kp = I ω² had been applied with I as the rotor inertia, while the inertia the joint actually accelerates is 2–84× larger. An intended 7 Hz critically-damped servo is really 0.77 Hz at ζ = 0.22 — strongly underdamped, and exactly the 1 Hz resonance visible in the before/after plots. The same formula is in the upstream simulator's own asset config.

Which numbers to believe

A fitted number is only a measurement if the data could determine it. Three checks, all independent of the optimiser's own cost:

Left/right symmetry
The two legs use identical motors, so mirrored joints must match. Damping agrees within 14% — a real measurement. Rotor inertia disagrees by 6–60× — noise. Two legs measuring the same physical quantity and landing that far apart settles it.
Refitting under different settings
Coulomb friction came out 0.001, 0.115, 0.18, 1.16 and 0.53 N·m across five fits — three orders of magnitude — with under 3% change in predictive accuracy. Unidentifiable. So I measured it a different way: hang the robot, drop all torque, and record which gravity torque each joint holds before it slides. 0.16–0.85 N·m, against the model's flat 0.3 guess.
Held-out scoring
Fitting link masses improved training error and made held-out error worse than doing nothing. The cause is exact collinearity: rotor inertia and link inertia are the same column of the Jacobian, so adding one makes the system singular.

That analysis is what sizes the domain randomisation for the RL: ±5% on the parameter I could pin down, 0.2–2.0 N·m on the one I couldn't. Identification centres the distribution; randomisation covers what's left. Randomising around a biased centre doesn't help — a systematic 18% torque error points the same way every sample, so the real robot sits outside the training distribution no matter how wide you make the rest.

Four things I got wrong

Each proposed, then killed by a measurement rather than abandoned.

Unmodelled actuator delay
Fitted a delay parameter; it landed in the interior and the cost got worse.
A mechanically jammed ankle
Built on the same number appearing in two experiments. It was under-driven: 0.101 → 0.652 rad with more amplitude.
High-frequency excitation for inertia
Constant amplitude gives acceleration ∝ f² — true, and useless at four times the real servo bandwidth.
Overestimated link inertia
Rejected on held-out data and on parameters pinning at their bounds.

On the robot

PPO in MuJoCo on the identified model, 32k iterations, then deployed over the Unitree SDK at 50 Hz. The policy's entire input is IMU and joint encoders — no state estimator, no contact sensors, no terrain scan, because none of those exist on the hardware. It stands for a minute with torques at 3–31% of limits, and walks at 0.6 m/s.

It also runs with 60–80 ms of measured command latency against the 0–20 ms it was trained for — three to four times the mismatch, which it tolerates better than I predicted.

What this doesn't cover

Flat ground, with a hoist strap slack as a catch. Every parameter was measured with the base welded and the legs hanging, so it covers joint and actuator dynamics and says nothing about contact modelling — which is the largest untested part of the model. Below roughly 0.4 m/s the policy deliberately stands still: the tracking reward makes standing worth 85% of maximum at a 0.2 m/s command. That's the reward function, not a failure.
